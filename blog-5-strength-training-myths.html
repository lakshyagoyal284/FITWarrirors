5 Strength Training Myths That Need To Retire

From a coach who actually competes in powerlifting.

I've heard all of these — in gyms, from clients, at family dinners. As someone who trains for strength and competes, let me put these five myths to rest properly.

Myth 1: "Lifting makes women bulky"

If it were that easy, every man in your gym would be huge. Muscle growth is slow and deliberate — months and years of progressive overload, consistent protein and food surplus. Women have significantly less testosterone, so building "bulky" muscles accidentally is basically impossible. What lifting actually gives women first: strength, posture, bone density, and that "toned" look everyone chases. The toned look is muscle.

Myth 2: "Muscle turns into fat when you stop"

Muscle and fat are different tissues — one cannot convert into the other, any more than a car turns into a bicycle. What actually happens when people stop training: muscle shrinks (atrophy) and, if eating stays the same while activity drops, fat increases. Two separate processes. The fix is simple — never fully stop. Reduce, yes; stop, no.

Myth 3: "You need to train every day for results"

Training breaks muscle down; recovery builds it up. Strength gains happen between sessions — sleep, protein, rest days. Three to four focused sessions a week beats seven exhausted ones. Consistency over months beats intensity over days.

Myth 4: "Cardio is better for fat loss"

Cardio burns calories during the session; strength training builds muscle that burns calories 24/7. For fat loss specifically, the combo wins — but if you only pick one, lifting preserves muscle while you lose fat, so what's left behind is shape and strength, not just a smaller version of the same body. That's exactly why my programs pair full-body strength work with diet — not hours of cardio.

Myth 5: "Start with light weights and endless reps to 'tone'"

"Toning" isn't a thing muscles do — there's only building muscle and losing fat. High-rep pink-dumbbell workouts don't "lengthen" or "tone" muscles. To change your body, lift weights that are genuinely challenging in the 8–15 rep range, progress the load over time, and let your diet reveal the shape. Heavy is relative: challenge is what matters, not a number.

💡 The truth is boring: lift progressively, eat enough protein, sleep, repeat. Everything else is marketing.

The Bottom Line

Strength training is the closest thing fitness has to a cheat code — for fat loss, confidence, health and capability. Don't let 1990s gym folklore keep you away from the racks.
